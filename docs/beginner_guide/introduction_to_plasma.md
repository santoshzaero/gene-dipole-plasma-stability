# Plasma and magnetic confinement

Plasma is a gas with freely moving charged particles. It can contain electrons and ions, or electrons and their positively charged counterparts, positrons. Because those particles respond to electric and magnetic fields and also generate fields, the motion of one part of the plasma can affect another. Plasmas occur in stars and in laboratory fusion experiments. They also power electric spacecraft thrusters.

Confinement is the effort to keep particles and heat in a desired region. A magnetic field bends the paths of moving charged particles. In a smooth field a particle can spiral around a field line while also moving along it. It can still drift across field lines, and collisions and fluctuations can help redistribute particles and heat.

A magnetic dipole can be made by driving current through a circular loop. In a cross-section, field lines curve around the current-carrying ring and close on themselves. Field strength changes along a line, which can cause some particles to bounce between regions of stronger field. The geometry gives a controlled setting for studying the interaction of particle motion, curvature, and plasma gradients. | Hydrogen ion | +1 | About 1,836 electron masses |
| Deuterium ion | +1 | About twice the hydrogen-ion mass |

Opposite charges reverse the sense of gyromotion. At the same temperature, a heavier species typically moves more slowly and has a larger gyroradius than a lighter species of the same charge magnitude. These differences can change how each species responds to a wave. In an electron–positron plasma the masses match, so the two species have a symmetry that the electron–ion mixtures lack.
